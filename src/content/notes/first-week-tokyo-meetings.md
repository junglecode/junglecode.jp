Your first week of meetings in Tokyo will probably feel slower and quieter than you're used to. That's normal. Most of what decides the outcome happens after you leave the room, and a few habits make it more likely that those later conversations go your way.

Don't be surprised if more people show up than you invited. A first meeting with a large company may include a senior person, the manager who'd own the project and the people who'd evaluate it or do the work. Each of them is there because they'll have a voice later in nemawashi (根回し), the informal consensus-building that happens before any formal decision. Seating and speaking order tend to follow rank, and JETRO notes that the most senior person often speaks last. Your hosts will show you where to sit. Don't direct your pitch only at whoever speaks the best English.

Bring more meishi (名刺), business cards, than you think you'll need, ideally with Japanese on one side and English on the other. Cards are exchanged at the start, beginning with the most senior people. Hold yours with both hands, with the text facing the other person, and say your name and company as you offer it. When you receive a card, read it before you put it down. Keep the cards on the table in front of you for the whole meeting, and if you line them up to match where people are sitting, you'll keep the names straight. Don't write on a card or put it in your back pocket.

Leave room for silence. When a question gets a long pause, let it sit. JETRO's guide gives several reasons people stay quiet in multicultural meetings. Many aren't confident speaking English, interrupting feels impolite and people want to give a complete, reliable answer, which takes time. If you fill every pause, you'll end up answering your own questions and missing what they would have said.

Say less than you planned to. Use short sentences with one idea at a time, and put numbers on the slide so nobody has to catch them by ear. Drop the superlatives. Anything you claim may end up in someone's internal summary for colleagues who weren't there, so say only what you'd be comfortable seeing quoted.

Bring Japanese-language materials. Japan ranked 96th of 123 countries in EF's 2025 English Proficiency Index. Even when the person across the table speaks English well, the colleagues who'll review your proposal may prefer to read Japanese. JETRO recommends sending written materials well before the meeting, so send the deck and a short Japanese summary ahead and bring printed copies.

If you're working with an interpreter, brief them a day ahead with your deck, your product terms and what you want from the meeting. With consecutive interpretation everything gets said twice, so plan to cover about half of what you'd cover in English. Speak in short segments, pause for the interpretation and keep your eyes on the person you're talking to.

## The decision won't happen in the meeting

Don't push for an answer. JETRO's guide warns that pressing for an immediate response may only get you tatemae (建前), the official position, rather than what people think. It also notes that formal meetings are often where decisions already reached through nemawashi get announced. A first meeting is where people decide whether to start those internal conversations about you. What you want at the end is a clear next step with a name and a date attached.

## The follow-up that counts

Send a written summary the same day or the next morning. Keep it short and factual, in Japanese and English if you can. Cover what you heard, what you agreed, the open questions and the next steps with owners and dates. Your counterpart may forward it internally, so write it for people who weren't in the room. JETRO's guide recommends confirming meeting results in writing, and it's the cheapest way to show you'll be easy to work with.

Then there's kentō shimasu (検討します), "we will consider it." Sometimes it means exactly that, and your proposal is going into an internal process. Sometimes it's a polite way to close the topic without saying no. JETRO's guide notes that if nothing follows "we will consider it," the answer may be no. You can tell the difference by asking about the process instead of the answer. Ask what they'd need from you to evaluate it, who else should see it and when it would make sense to check in. If those questions get concrete answers, you're in a process. If they don't, treat it as a no for now and keep the relationship warm.

The first week is mostly about earning the second meeting. Be on time, keep it short, write things down and follow up fast.
