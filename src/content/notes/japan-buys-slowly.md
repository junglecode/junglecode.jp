If you're bringing an AI product to large Japanese companies, the first thing to recalibrate is your sense of time. The sales cycle you planned around in the US will likely run longer here. That's rarely a sign the deal is dying. It's how decisions get made, and the same process is the reason customers who say yes tend to stay.

Most of the time goes into building agreement. Large companies often formalize a decision through ringi (稟議), the written approval document that circulates for sign-off. It starts lower in the organization and moves up a set route, and the people affected by the decision review it and add their seal. JETRO's guide for foreign businesspeople notes that even companies without a formal ringi tend to decide in a similar way.

Before that document exists, there's nemawashi (根回し), the informal, one-on-one conversations that build support before anything is proposed formally. The word comes from gardening, where it means preparing a tree's roots before it's moved. In a company, it means the person sponsoring your proposal has already talked with IT, security, legal, finance and the business owner, and has worked through their concerns. By the time there's a formal meeting, the decision has often been made.

Risk aversion stretches this further. Nobody wants their seal on the approval for a vendor that later caused a data incident or left the market. For an AI company, the questions are predictable. Where is the data stored? Is it used to train your models? Who's accountable when the model gets something wrong? Will you still be here in three years? Each reviewer on the ringi route has to be comfortable with those answers before signing.

Then there's the PoC trap. Japanese companies run a lot of proofs of concept, or PoCs, and many of them never reach production. The Japanese tech press has names for it, PoC-domari (PoC止まり), getting stuck at the PoC stage, and PoC-zukare (PoC疲れ), the fatigue that sets in after too many pilots. A pilot can meet every technical goal and still go nowhere, because nobody agreed up front on what success meant, who would pay for production or which budget it would come from.

## Why they stay

The slow part buys you something. JETRO points out that a long, consensus-based decision tends to make implementation smoother, because everyone affected already knows about the decision and most of them agree with it. Once you're in, you're part of how the organization works. Replacing you would take another ringi, another round of nemawashi and someone explaining why the original decision should be reversed. Most organizations would rather keep working with a vendor that keeps showing up.

That loyalty comes with expectations. JETRO's guide quotes a common phrase, okyakusama wa kamisama desu (お客様は神様です), "the customer is god," and describes how Japanese customers expect a vendor to make a visible effort on their requests, including ones a head office overseas might consider minor. If your support model treats Japan as a lower priority than your home market, your customers will notice.

## What to do differently

Plan for the long cycle. Set your Japan runway, hiring and targets around a longer sales cycle, and measure the team on progress through the customer's process, not only on revenue closed in the first two quarters. Many large Japanese companies run an April-to-March fiscal year, and more than 2,000 companies listed in Tokyo close their books in March. Ask early when next year's budget gets drafted, because a project that isn't in the plan has to find money somewhere else.

Find the internal champion. You need one person inside the company who wants this to happen and is willing to carry the ringi. Your job is to make their work easier. Ask who needs to agree, in what order and what each of them is worried about. Then give your champion the answers in writing before anyone asks.

Design the pilot with a written path to production. Before the pilot starts, agree in writing on the success criteria, the data and security terms that will apply in production, the production pricing, who owns the budget and the date when both sides decide. If the pilot meets the criteria, most of the production ringi should already be written.

Bring Japanese-language materials and support commitments. Your champion has to explain your product to people who'll never meet you, so give them a proposal, security and data documentation and product materials in Japanese. Put your support commitments in writing too, including Japanese-language support, response times and coverage during Japanese business hours.

None of this makes the cycle short. It makes it predictable, and when the decision comes, the whole organization has already agreed to it. That's worth planning around.
